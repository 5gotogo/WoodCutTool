Plywood Yield Percentage Explained

Plywood yield percentage is finished-part area divided by the total nominal or usable area of the sheets assigned to the layout, multiplied by 100. It is a useful comparison metric when scenarios use the same rules, but it does not measure cut safety, grain quality, defect avoidance, offcut usefulness, price, or whether every part was successfully placed. Always read the denominator and completion status before comparing percentages.

Calculate the basic percentage

Add the area of every finished part, including quantity. Divide that total by the area of all sheets in the scenario and multiply by 100. If parts total 3,200 square inches on one nominal 48 × 96 sheet, nominal-area yield is 69.4%. If the plan uses a measured 47.5 × 95.5 inch rectangle after trim, usable-area yield is 70.5%. Both calculations can be correct because they answer slightly different questions.

Name the denominator

Nominal-area yield helps compare purchasing formats because it uses the full panel area bought. Usable-area yield evaluates how tightly parts fill the clean rectangle after declared trim. Neither should silently exclude rejected parts or defects. Write “nominal sheet yield” or “usable-area yield” in reports and project notes so a change in edge-trim policy cannot look like an improvement in nesting.

Why average yield can mislead

WoodCutTool's 36-project benchmark reported a 57.9% unweighted mean project yield with rotation allowed. That does not mean 42.1% of every purchase becomes trash. Some projects leave a large reusable rectangle; others leave fragmented strips. An unweighted mean also gives a one-sheet project the same influence as a multi-sheet project. Use the underlying rows when making a project decision.

Completion comes before efficiency

A partial layout can show a deceptively strong percentage because an oversized or constrained part was rejected. Confirm that the rejected-piece count is zero and every required quantity appears before reading yield. The sheet-format dataset marks complete layouts for exactly this reason. A 90% result with one missing cabinet side is not more efficient than a 60% result that produces the complete project.

Useful offcuts are not waste

The wood waste calculator can price leftover area, but the business value depends on shape, grade, thickness, labeling, and likelihood of reuse. Define a minimum offcut size for the shop, label retained pieces, and track whether they are consumed. A layout that preserves one square offcut may be operationally better than a slightly higher-yield layout that creates narrow pieces below the reuse threshold.

Compare scenarios fairly

Keep parts, quantities, stock definition, kerf, trim, rotation rules, and defect policy constant when comparing algorithms or cut orders. Then review sheet count, yield, offcut shapes, number of cuts, handling, and sequence together. Save candidate layouts in CutList and choose the plan that is complete and practical, not simply the one with the largest percentage printed at the top.
