Why Does My Plywood Cut Layout Not Fit?

A plywood cut layout can fail even when the total part area is smaller than the total sheet area. Rectangles need the right shapes of free space, every part must fit within the stock boundaries, and real cuts need kerf and usable-edge allowances. Troubleshoot the inputs in a fixed order so you correct the actual constraint instead of deleting safety margins or rotating visible parts at random.

Check for a single oversized part

Compare the width and length of every part with the usable stock dimensions in both permitted orientations. One tall cabinet side can invalidate an otherwise efficient small-sheet layout. The sheet-format benchmark recorded rejected-piece counts explicitly because nine of 36 project inputs could not fully fit on modeled 5 × 5 panels. A layout that packs the remaining parts is still incomplete.

Verify units, quantities, and duplicates

A millimeter value entered as inches creates an obvious failure, but subtler mistakes are common: width and length reversed, a quantity copied twice, a material assigned to the wrong group, or finished dimensions mixed with rough-cut dimensions. Sort the cut list by material and size, total repeated parts, and compare the result with the drawing or cabinet schedule. Fix the source list before changing the optimizer settings.

Confirm usable stock and allowances

Measure the real panel and subtract any required perimeter trim to establish the usable rectangle. Enter the measured saw kerf separately. If a layout fits only with nominal 48 × 96 stock, zero trim, and zero kerf, it is not robust enough for a real sheet. Read the usable plywood size guide and rerun the plan with explicit assumptions.

Review rotation and grain locks

An orientation-locked part may fit by dimensions but fail to fit the remaining free rectangles. Do not simply enable rotation for everything. Mark visible faces, grain direction, overlays, and machining requirements, then allow rotation only for parts that remain valid after turning. WoodCutTool's rotation comparison found five of 36 test projects used an extra sheet when orientation was locked, showing that the constraint can legitimately change the purchase estimate.

Look for geometric fragmentation

Area-only math treats every leftover square inch as interchangeable. A real layout can leave several narrow or disconnected spaces that add up to enough area but cannot accept the next rectangle. Try a different part order, prioritize the largest or most constrained pieces, test another sheet size, or group repeated parts differently. If the project still needs another panel, accept the geometric result rather than forcing dimensions to fit the estimate.

Use a controlled fallback sequence

In CutList, duplicate the project before experimenting. First correct data errors. Second test only valid rotations. Third compare locally available stock sizes. Fourth consider a small design change that does not compromise structure, hardware clearance, or appearance. Last, add a sheet and inspect whether the offcut is useful. Change one assumption at a time and name each scenario so the final layout remains explainable.
